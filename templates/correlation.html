Sentiment vs Volatility Correlation

Pearson correlation between daily sentiment (from news) and rolling volatility (from prices). Inspired by Halouskova and Lyocsa (2025): sentiment helps explain volatility; Kubica et al. (2025): finance-specific NLP is required.

Note: live news are recent headlines; volatility is computed from historical candles, so the overlap may be limited on short samples.

What this page measures (no model, pure statistics)

This tab does not use any ML model. For every trading day we take two numbers and check how they relate:

  • X = daily sentiment: the average FinBERT polarity of that day's headlines (-1 very negative to +1 very positive).
  • Y = volatility: the 20-day rolling volatility computed from prices that day.

Each dot in the scatter below is one trading day (X, Y). The line charts show the same two series over time. We then compute the Pearson correlation between X and Y.

Pearson r (from -1 to +1) shows the direction and strength of the linear link: +1 move perfectly together, -1 move perfectly opposite, 0 no linear link. Rough scale: |r| below 0.1 negligible, 0.1 to 0.3 weak, 0.3 to 0.5 moderate, above 0.5 strong.

p-value tests whether the correlation is real or just a fluke. Imagine sentiment and volatility were truly unrelated: the p-value is the probability that random data would still produce a correlation at least as strong as the one we measured. So a smaller p-value is more trustworthy. The usual cut-off is 0.05 (we accept at most a 5% risk of a false alarm): p below 0.05 means the link is significant and considered real; p of 0.05 or more means it is not significant and may be a coincidence (common with few observations or a calm period).
